# Example: Dusk — Consumer Wellness Brand

This example shows the Branding Wizard working through a brand build for a DTC
consumer wellness company. Contrast with the Vaultly B2B SaaS example for a sense
of how the same frameworks produce fundamentally different creative directions.

---

## User Input

> "We're launching Dusk — a magnesium supplement brand focused on sleep quality.
> DTC, $39/month subscription. Targeting high-performing professionals, 28-42,
> who've tried melatonin and hated how groggy it made them. We want to feel different
> from clinical supplement brands (all-white, medical-looking) and from the lavender-
> everything-soft-light wellness aesthetic. Competitors: Moon Juice, Olly, Calm (app).
> We love: Liquid Death (tone), Aesop (product aesthetic), Athletic Greens (premium
> supplement positioning). We definitely don't want: crystals, affirmations, 'self-care
> sundays,' woo-woo anything."

---

## Step 1: Brand Tension

**Tension: High Performance / Rest**

> "Dusk sits in a contradiction that's genuinely interesting: the customers who need
> sleep most — high-performers, ambitious people, executives — are culturally conditioned
> to treat rest as weakness. 'I'll sleep when I'm dead.' 'I run on 5 hours.' Sleep
> optimization exists for people who refuse to admit they need sleep.
>
> Your brand tension is: High Performance / Rest. You're not a wellness brand — you're
> a performance brand whose product happens to be sleep. The competitor that gets closest
> to this is Eight Sleep (the $3,000 mattress that tracks your HRV). But you're
> accessible at $39/month.
>
> The creative implication: Dusk should feel like it was designed for someone who reads
> Naval Ravikant and wears Lululemon, not someone who lights candles and follows moon
> cycles. The visual language should feel precise and performance-adjacent, not soft and
> spiritual. The copy should treat sleep like a competitive advantage, not a form of
> self-care."

---

## Step 2: Category Villain

**Villain: The Wellness Industrial Complex's Infantilization of Rest**

> "The supplement wellness industry has been telling high-performers that caring about
> their recovery makes them soft — or alternatively, wrapping sleep in so much lavender
> and crystal energy that anyone with a type-A personality would rather be groggy than
> be seen using it.
>
> Dusk exists because 'sleep hygiene' shouldn't sound like something your yoga teacher
> invented, and supplementing your recovery shouldn't require buying into an aesthetic
> that has nothing to do with how you actually live.
>
> Villain statement: 'Dusk exists because the wellness industry has been making
> high-performers choose between performance culture and actual rest for too long.'"

---

## Step 3: Archetype Diagnosis

**Primary: Sage (70%) — Secondary: Outlaw (30%)**

> "Dusk is a Sage brand with an Outlaw streak. Here's what that specific combination
> produces:
>
> The Sage brings: precision, evidence-based claims, intellectual credibility. The brand
> earns trust through mechanism — it explains how magnesium actually works, cites the
> specific research, trusts the customer to be smart enough to care about the mechanism.
>
> The Outlaw brings: the refusal to look or sound like the wellness category. The implicit
> 'we're not that' in every design decision. The willingness to say something the industry
> won't — like 'melatonin is probably making your sleep worse, not better.'
>
> Together: Dusk sounds like a doctor who's also done their time in performance culture
> and refuses to talk to you like you need hand-holding. It looks nothing like a supplement
> brand. It looks like something you'd find next to your Kindle and your cold brew."

---

## Step 4: Positioning

**Onliness Statement:**
> "Dusk is the only sleep supplement designed for high-performers who refuse to call it
> wellness — for people who want better recovery without buying into an aesthetic they don't
> recognize themselves in."

**Against / For / Beyond:**

| Dimension | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Against** | Not lavender. Not crystals. Not melatonin. Not the supplement that makes you feel like you're taking care of yourself — the one that makes you actually recover. |
| **For** | For the person who treats their 7 hours of sleep the same way they treat their workout: as an input to performance, not a retreat from ambition. |
| **Beyond** | What it looks like when the most analytically rigorous people in the world take sleep seriously. Not as self-care — as the competitive advantage they've been skipping. |

**Visual Direction Scores:**

```
Dusk target:        Minimal(2) / Cool(3) / Serious(3) / Novel(3) / Abstract(3)
Moon Juice:         Expressive(4) / Warm(2) / Playful(3) / Novel(3) / Abstract(4)
Olly:               Expressive(4) / Warm(2) / Playful(2) / Familiar(2) / Literal(2)
Athletic Greens:    Minimal(2) / Warm(3) / Serious(3) / Familiar(2) / Literal(2)

Whitespace: Nobody in this category owns Cool + Serious + Minimal. Every competitor
            is warm or playful or both. Dusk can be the only cool-toned, precise,
            unapologetically serious sleep brand.
```

---

## Step 5: Messaging

**Category Claim:** Performance recovery for people who hate the word 'wellness.'

**Brand Promise:** Better sleep, without feeling like you've joined a cult.

**Proof Points:**
1. Magnesium glycinate at clinical dose (300mg) → the form your body actually absorbs,
   at the amount that affects sleep architecture — not the underdosed 50mg in most
   supplements
2. No melatonin → No morning grogginess, no dependency, no suppression of your natural
   melatonin production
3. Works with your chronobiology, not against it → enhances the sleep you already have
   instead of forcing sedation

**Taglines:**

1. "Sleep like it's your edge." — Outcome-first. Performance language. **Strong.**
   Best for: paid social, homepage hero, brand-level positioning.

2. "No melatonin. No nonsense. No grogginess." — Enemy-first. Direct comparison to
   the dominant competitor (melatonin). **Strong.**
   Best for: performance marketing, comparison ads.

3. "For people who don't own a diffuser." — Identity-first. Exclusion as inclusion.
   **Solid.** Best for: brand ads, social media bio, merchandise.

4. "Recovery, not ritual." — Category-first. Names the new category (performance
   recovery) while rejecting the old one (wellness ritual). **Strong.**
   Best for: homepage, investor narrative, brand story.

5. "What happens when you stop fighting your biology." — Provocation. Indicts
   the hustle culture that treats sleep as optional. **Worth testing.**
   Best for: email subject lines, awareness-stage social.

**Recommended primary: "Sleep like it's your edge."** — Three words, passes the
billboard test, uses performance language the audience respects, implies the benefit
without describing the supplement.

**Voice:**
- Formal(2) ↔ Casual: Smart casual. Writes like a knowledgeable friend.
- Reserved(2) ↔ Enthusiastic: Quietly confident. Never exclamation points.
- Serious(3) ↔ Humorous: Dry wit is in-brand. Earnest wellness language is not.
- Technical(2) ↔ Accessible: Explains mechanism — but plainly. "Your body converts
  this form" not "bioavailability metrics."

---

## Step 6: Visual Direction

**Color System:**
| Role | Hex | Emotional function |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | #1A1B2E | Midnight — the depth of actual sleep, not the pastel suggestion of it |
| Secondary | #C8B8A2 | Warm sand — a single warm note that keeps it from feeling clinical |
| Accent | #E8E0D5 | Near-white — used for type on dark backgrounds, CTA text |
| Background light | #F5F2EE | Off-white with warmth — never pure white |
| Text primary | #1A1B2E | Same as primary |
| Text secondary | #8B8577 | Muted warm grey |

**Typography:**
- Headlines: Canela (elegant serif with slight editorial weight) — weight 300-400
- Body: Suisse Int'l (precise, slightly cool sans-serif) — not Inter, too generic
- UI/callouts: Suisse Int'l Mono for supplement facts, dosage numbers

**Imagery direction:**
- Photography treatment: Near-dark, cool blue-grey tones at edges warming to amber
  in center — the visual feel of a room transitioning from night to morning
- Subject matter: Environmental — objects in low-light rooms, not people; the spaces
  where people recover rather than the people recovering
- What to never show: crystals, candles, lavender, women in white robes, yoga poses,
  anyone doing anything that looks vaguely spiritual or soft, stock photos of "sleep"
  (invariably cheesy)

**3 References:**
1. **Aesop** — Steal: the museum-like product presentation, the density of product
   information, the assumption that the customer is intelligent. Skip: the warmth
   (too artisanal; Dusk is more precise, less handmade-feeling)
2. **Headspace (the original design era)** — Steal: how they made an abstract concept
   (meditation) feel tangible and specific through illustration and consistent visual
   system. Skip: the coral and orange palette — too warm for Dusk's territory
3. **Eight Sleep** — Steal: performance language applied to sleep, data as proof,
   the product shot as precision object. Skip: the everything-tech aesthetic (Dusk
   is warmer and more human than Eight Sleep's near-clinical feel)

---

## Why This Works

1. **The tension is ownable.** No supplement brand is currently in the "performance
   recovery, anti-wellness" territory. The category either goes clinical (which feels
   cold) or spiritual (which alienates the target). Dusk found the gap.

2. **The Sage/Outlaw combination produces a genuinely distinctive voice.** When you
   apply Outlaw provocation through a Sage's precision, you get something that feels
   like "the supplement brand that finally tells you the truth about melatonin." That
   tone is believable AND differentiating.

3. **The visual direction is immediately distinguishable.** Put Dusk next to Moon Juice,
   Olly, and Athletic Greens. The near-dark primary, the warm sand secondary, the serif
   headline treatment — nothing in the category looks like this. The Competitor Swap Test
   passes immediately.

4. **The "For people who don't own a diffuser" positioning does double work.** It tells
   the target customer "this is for you" (identity recognition) and tells the non-target
   "this isn't for you" (appropriate exclusion). The best positioning repels as clearly
   as it attracts.
