# The Category Villain Protocol

## The Insight

Every great brand has an enemy. Not a competitor — an enemy. A philosophy, a behavior,
a way of doing things that the brand exists to destroy.

Naming the enemy is more powerful than naming your features because:
1. It creates a story (hero vs. villain) rather than a statement (we do X)
2. It recruits customers as protagonists — they're not buying a product, they're joining a side
3. It creates natural differentiation — if your enemy is what your competitors do, you can't look like them
4. It's emotionally charged — people don't buy things they believe in as strongly as they buy against things they hate

The villain is not your competitor. The villain is what your competitor represents, enables, or refuses to fix.

---

## Villain vs. Competitor

| Brand | Competitor | Villain |
|---|---|---|
| Mailchimp | Constant Contact | Stuffy, corporate email marketing |
| Slack | Email | The infinite inbox that owns your day |
| Liquid Death | Evian, Smartwater | Boring environmentally-destructive plastic |
| Dollar Shave Club | Gillette | The razor industry's hostage pricing |
| Patagonia | North Face (sort of) | Disposable consumption culture |
| Stripe | PayPal, Braintree | Payment integration as punishment |
| Linear | Jira | Project management that creates work about work |
| Notion | Confluence, Google Docs | Tools that fragment your thinking |
| Oatly | Dairy industry (sort of) | An industry's grip on what food is allowed to be |

Notice: in almost every case, the villain is more interesting and more energizing than
the named competitor. The competitor is a company. The villain is a behavior that customers
already hate — they just haven't had language for it yet.

## Finding the Villain

### Step 1: Audit the category's sins
What does every player in this category do that makes customers feel stupid, trapped,
disrespected, or overcharged? What does the category assume is acceptable that shouldn't be?

Run this diagnostic:
- What do customers complain about that competitors treat as inevitable?
- What does "the way it's always been done" in this category cost customers in time, money, or dignity?
- What would customers celebrate if it disappeared tomorrow?

### Step 2: Name the behavior, not the company
The villain should be a behavior, attitude, or system — not a named company. Named
companies change, get acquired, go bankrupt. Behaviors persist. And a behavior-villain
makes it clear that any company practicing that behavior is the enemy — even future ones.

**Too specific (company as villain):** "We're not like Salesforce"
**Too vague (too abstract):** "We're fighting inefficiency"
**Right (behavior as villain):** "We're ending the era of enterprise software that treats users as an afterthought"

### Step 3: Make the customer the hero
The villain exists so the customer can be the hero. The brand is the weapon, the ally,
the mentor — not the protagonist. Get this wrong and you become the thing you're fighting:
a brand that's more interested in talking about itself than in solving the problem.

**Wrong framing:** "We're fighting old-school finance software"
**Right framing:** "Finance teams who care about craft shouldn't have to use software designed in 2003"

## Villain Archetypes

### The Gatekeeper
Keeps something from the people who deserve it. Usually: access, information, tools,
pricing, respect. The brand exists to tear down the gate.

Examples: Robinhood (finance), Wikipedia (knowledge), Duolingo (language education),
Stripe (payment infrastructure), Figma (professional design tools)

**Villain language:** "Built for the few." "Only if you can afford the agency." "Enterprise only."
**Hero language:** "Built for anyone who builds." "Professional tools, zero gatekeeping."

### The Exploiter
Takes advantage of customers' lack of alternatives, information, or time. Usually:
hidden fees, proprietary lock-in, dark patterns, predatory pricing.

Examples: Dollar Shave Club (razor industry), Robinhood (again), BrewDog (early days),
direct-to-consumer brands generally

**Villain language:** "The subscription you forgot to cancel." "The fee they don't tell you about."
**Hero language:** "No tricks. No lock-in." "One price. Everything included."

### The Complicator
Makes things unnecessarily complex to create dependency, justify headcount, or cover
inadequacy. Usually: enterprise software, legal/finance/medical categories, legacy systems.

Examples: Stripe (payment complexity), Linear (project management complexity), Notion
(knowledge management fragmentation), Loom (meeting complexity)

**Villain language:** "Let me loop in three more people to answer that." "That'll take six weeks to implement."
**Hero language:** "You could have done this in ten minutes." "One tool. One source of truth."

### The Dignitarian
The category that treats a mainstream behavior as shameful, niche, or second-class.

Examples: Liquid Death (health = boring), Glossier (beauty = performance, not expression),
Bumble (dating apps that treat women as passive)

**Villain language:** "For people who care about that sort of thing." "If you're into healthy options..."
**Hero language:** "You shouldn't have to choose between [X] and [Y]." "This is just what [behavior] looks like now."

### The Time Thief
Steals the most valuable resource. Usually: outdated workflows, bad tooling, unnecessary
meetings, manual processes.

Examples: Notion, Loom, Slack, Calendly, Zapier, every "automation" SaaS

**Villain language:** "Could you just send me a quick email about that?"
**Hero language:** "Get Thursday back."

## The Villain Statement

Once identified, express the villain in one sentence. This becomes the invisible spine
of all creative direction — not for external use, but for internal decision-making.

**Format:** "[Brand] exists because [the villain] has been [doing X to customers] for too long."

**Examples:**
- "Vaultly exists because AP software has been treating finance teams as data-entry clerks for too long."
- "Liquid Death exists because the beverage industry has been boring people into plastic for too long."
- "Linear exists because project management tools have been creating work about work for too long."

This sentence should make the founding team feel something. If it feels like a press release,
it's not specific enough. If someone on the team looks uncomfortable, you're in the right territory.

---

## Using the Villain in Creative Work

**Homepage hero:** Don't open with what you do. Open with what you're ending.
- Linear: "The issue tracker you'll actually enjoy using" (the villain is the issue tracker you don't)
- Loom: "Say it with video" (the villain is the meeting that could have been a video)
- Notion: "The all-in-one workspace" (the villain is the 12 tools it replaces)

**Ad creative:** The villain drives the conflict. The product provides the resolution.
- DSC's launch video: The villain is the razor industry, personified as absurd and exploitative. Michael Dubin is the resolution.
- Patagonia's "Don't Buy This Jacket": The villain is consumption culture. Patagonia is the resolution (buy less, buy right).

**Content strategy:** The villain gives you an inexhaustible content topic — the thing
you're fighting is always doing something worth calling out.

**Sales:** When the prospect names a pain, connect it to the villain. "That's exactly
the thing we're here to end." The sale becomes a recruitment into the cause.

---

## The Villain Test

Before writing any copy or designing any touchpoint, ask:
1. Does this make the villain visible?
2. Does this make the customer the hero?
3. Does this make our brand the weapon/ally that makes winning possible?

If the answer to all three is no, the work is too focused on the brand. Fix it.
