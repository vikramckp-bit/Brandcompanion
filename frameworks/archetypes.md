# Brand Archetype Matrix

## How to Use This

Archetypes are diagnostic tools, not personality quizzes. The goal is not to "find your
archetype" — it's to use archetype language to make creative decisions faster and more
consistent.

Every brand maps to 1 primary archetype (70% of expression) + 1 secondary (30%).
The combination creates specificity. The same primary archetype feels completely different
depending on the secondary. A Hero brand with a Jester secondary (Old Spice) is nothing
like a Hero brand with a Sage secondary (IBM).

**The combination IS the brand.** Single-archetype brands are forgettable. It's the
specific flavor of the pairing that creates distinctiveness.

After diagnosing, present as:
> "[Brand] is a [Primary] with a [Secondary] streak — meaning [1-2 sentences on what
> this specific combination creates visually and verbally]."

## The 12 Archetypes

### 1. The Innocent
**Core desire:** Safety, simplicity, happiness
**Brand belief:** The world is good. Optimism is a business strategy.

**Visual direction:** Light palettes (whites, creams, soft greens, sky blues). Generous
whitespace. Rounded forms — nothing angular or aggressive. Natural imagery: daylight,
outdoor settings, unposed moments. Sans-serif with open letterforms. Photography feels
candid and warm.

**Verbal direction:** Short sentences. Optimistic but never naive — there's a difference
between innocent and ignorant. Avoids jargon. Speaks like a trusted friend who happens
to know everything about your problem.

**Danger zones:** Innocence reads as childish when overdone. Don't confuse simplicity
with emptiness. The Innocent brand has substance — it just doesn't show off.

**Example brands:** Dove, Aveeno, Oatly (in their simpler executions), early Airbnb

**Interesting combinations:**
- Innocent + Jester = Self-aware optimism (Oatly)
- Innocent + Explorer = Pure adventure, nothing synthetic (Patagonia's product comms)
- Innocent + Creator = Beautiful simplicity with intentional craft (Muji)

---

### 2. The Explorer
**Core desire:** Freedom, discovery, authentic experience
**Brand belief:** The best life is lived off the beaten path.

**Visual direction:** Expansive imagery — landscapes, horizons, open space. Earth tones
mixed with high-saturation adventure accents. Rugged typography with character. Layouts
that feel uncontained — bleeds, asymmetry, photos that push past the frame. Photography
looks like it was shot on the move.

**Verbal direction:** Active verbs. Future-oriented. Speaks to possibility. "What if"
framing. Never corporate or polished — that's the opposite of exploration.

**Danger zones:** Don't become a travel blog. Don't use stock photography of people
looking into distances. Authenticity is everything to Explorer brands — a single
inauthentic visual choice collapses the whole thing.

**Example brands:** Patagonia, REI, Jeep, Arc'teryx, AllTrails

**Interesting combinations:**
- Explorer + Sage = The intellectual adventurer (Monocle magazine, Kinfolk)
- Explorer + Creator = Craft built for the wild (Yeti, Leatherman)
- Explorer + Outlaw = Radical freedom (Surly Bikes, Gnarly Nutrition)

---

### 3. The Sage
**Core desire:** Truth, knowledge, wisdom
**Brand belief:** The right information, applied correctly, changes everything.

**Visual direction:** Structured layouts. Strong information hierarchy. Serif or
sophisticated sans-serif typography. Muted/intellectual palette: navy, charcoal, cream,
forest green, warm grey. Data visualization as design element. Photography is composed
and deliberate — nothing accidental.

**Verbal direction:** Precise language. Evidence-based claims. Teaches without
condescension. Uses frameworks and mental models. Footnotes are not an embarrassment —
they're proof of rigor.

**Danger zones:** Sage brands die from dryness. Intelligence must be in service of the
reader, not proof of the brand's credentials. The moment a Sage brand starts showing off
its knowledge instead of giving it away, it becomes inaccessible.

**Example brands:** The Economist, Airtable, McKinsey (visual expression), Stripe Press

**Interesting combinations:**
- Sage + Creator = Knowledge as craft (Notion, Figma's documentation)
- Sage + Ruler = Authoritative intelligence (Bloomberg, The Atlantic)
- Sage + Jester = Smart that doesn't take itself too seriously (early Google, Adam Grant)

---

### 4. The Hero
**Core desire:** Mastery, courage, achievement
**Brand belief:** Greatness is earned. Every day is a test.

**Visual direction:** Bold contrasts. Strong geometric forms. Dynamic imagery showing
motion, effort, achievement. Condensed or impact typography. Color: red, black, gold,
white — or deep jewel tones. Nothing soft, nothing tentative.

**Verbal direction:** Imperative mood. Challenge-oriented. "You can." "We will." "Do it."
Short, punchy. Power words: achieve, conquer, build, own, master, win.

**Danger zones:** Hero brands tip into aggression when they mistake toughness for
hostility. The Hero is about achievement, not domination. When Hero brands start
talking about competitors instead of the customer's potential, they've gone wrong.

**Example brands:** Nike, Under Armour, BMW, FedEx (the campaign, not the company),
CrossFit

**Interesting combinations:**
- Hero + Creator = Achievement through craft (Nike + its design legacy)
- Hero + Sage = Disciplined excellence (Peloton instructor persona)
- Hero + Caregiver = Strength in service of others (Nurses, firefighter brands)

---

### 5. The Outlaw
**Core desire:** Revolution, liberation, disruption
**Brand belief:** The rules exist to be broken. The system is the problem.

**Visual direction:** High contrast. Dark palettes with explosive accents. Rough textures —
grain, distress, visible process. Edgy typography: condensed, distressed, unconventional
placement. Photography that's raw, unpolished, slightly uncomfortable.

**Verbal direction:** Provocative. Comfortable with controversy. Uses "them vs. us"
framing. Challenges assumptions directly. Short, punchy. The brand would rather be
argued about than ignored.

**Danger zones:** Outlaw brands fail in two ways: (1) They're edgy without substance —
style rebellion with no actual philosophy behind it. (2) They get successful and can no
longer credibly claim to be against the system they're now part of. Every Outlaw brand
has a moment of reckoning.

**Example brands:** Harley-Davidson, Liquid Death, Diesel (peak era), early Virgin,
Patagonia (in its activist mode)

**Interesting combinations:**
- Outlaw + Jester = Comic rebellion (Liquid Death, Dollar Shave Club)
- Outlaw + Creator = Anti-establishment craft (Supreme, A-COLD-WALL)
- Outlaw + Hero = Righteous disruption (early Tesla narrative, SpaceX)

---

### 6. The Magician
**Core desire:** Transformation, making dreams real
**Brand belief:** Reality is more malleable than you think.

**Visual direction:** Rich, immersive visuals. Deep purples, midnight blues, gradient
golds. Smooth transitions and animation. Typography that feels elevated but accessible.
The visual effect should feel like stepping into another world.

**Verbal direction:** Transformational language. Before/after framing. "Imagine."
"Transform." "What if." Confident without being arrogant. Doesn't explain the trick —
shows the result.

**Danger zones:** Magician brands overpromise constantly. "Change your life" is the
Magician's natural language, but without product proof behind it, it becomes noise —
or worse, manipulation. The Magician brand must deliver genuine transformation.

**Example brands:** Apple, Disney, Tesla, Dyson, Adobe

**Interesting combinations:**
- Magician + Sage = Transformation through knowledge (Coursera, MasterClass)
- Magician + Creator = Transformation through making (Adobe, Canva)
- Magician + Ruler = Transformative luxury (Apple at its premium peak)

---

### 7. The Everyperson
**Core desire:** Belonging, connection, being enough
**Brand belief:** The best things in life should be for everyone.

**Visual direction:** Warm, accessible palettes. Real photography of real people — not
models, not idealizations. Comfortable, readable typography — nothing intimidating.
Familiar layouts that don't require orientation. Feels like somewhere you've been before.

**Verbal direction:** Conversational. Uses "we" heavily. Relatable humor. No jargon.
No exclusivity signals. Speaks like a neighbor who happens to make great [product].

**Danger zones:** Everyperson brands become invisible when they're too focused on
being inoffensive. Accessibility is not the same as blandness. The best Everyperson
brands have a genuine point of view — they've just made it welcoming.

**Example brands:** IKEA, Target, Slack (in its early approachable phase), Shopify

**Interesting combinations:**
- Everyperson + Creator = Democratic creativity (IKEA, Canva, Figma community)
- Everyperson + Jester = Self-aware accessibility (Slack, MailChimp's early voice)
- Everyperson + Hero = Anyone-can-be-great (Peloton's customer-as-hero mode)

---

### 8. The Lover
**Core desire:** Intimacy, sensory pleasure, deep connection
**Brand belief:** Life is more beautiful when you pay attention to it.

**Visual direction:** Rich textures, warm palettes (burgundy, gold, deep rose, sage).
Serif or elegant script typography. Photography that's intimate, close-up, sensory.
Premium materials and surfaces. Layouts breathe but are never cold.

**Verbal direction:** Evocative, sensory language. Appeals to how things feel, smell,
taste. Invitational tone. "Experience." "Feel." "Discover." Second person used intimately.

**Danger zones:** Lover brands tip from intimate to exploitative when they prioritize
desire creation over genuine connection. The line between seduction and manipulation
is thin and must be respected.

**Example brands:** Chanel, Airbnb (at its best), Aesop, Haagen-Dazs, Glossier

**Interesting combinations:**
- Lover + Creator = Craft as sensory experience (Aesop, LUSH)
- Lover + Everyperson = Accessible pleasure (Airbnb's "belong anywhere")
- Lover + Sage = Informed desire (Wirecutter, Serious Eats)

---

### 9. The Jester
**Core desire:** Joy, humor, living in the moment
**Brand belief:** Taking yourself too seriously is the real tragedy.

**Visual direction:** Bright, saturated colors. Playful typography with varied weights,
unexpected placement, and personality. Illustration over photography where possible.
Motion and animation. Asymmetric layouts that feel energetic. Nothing static.

**Verbal direction:** Witty, surprising, self-aware. Earns its jokes — the humor
serves the message, not the other way around. Pop culture references used precisely.
Comfortable breaking the fourth wall.

**Danger zones:** Jester brands sacrifice clarity for cleverness constantly. If people
laugh but don't know what you sell, you've failed. The joke must make the point, not
replace it. Also: humor ages. What's funny in 2024 is cringe in 2027.

**Example brands:** Old Spice, Liquid Death, Dollar Shave Club, Oatly, Duolingo

**Interesting combinations:**
- Jester + Sage = Funny AND smart (Adam Grant, early Twitter brand)
- Jester + Outlaw = Rebellious comedy (Liquid Death, DSC)
- Jester + Caregiver = Warm humor (Mailchimp, Monzo bank)

---

### 10. The Caregiver
**Core desire:** Protecting, nurturing, serving others
**Brand belief:** Showing up for people is the highest purpose.

**Visual direction:** Warm, soft palettes (soft blue, sage, cream, warm white).
Rounded, approachable forms. Photography shows genuine human connection — real hands,
real faces, real moments. Generous spacing that feels considerate, not empty.

**Verbal direction:** Empathetic. Second person. "We're here for you." Reassuring
without being condescending. Strength expressed through service.

**Danger zones:** Caregiver brands become weakness signals when they over-emphasize
vulnerability. The strongest Caregiver brands lead with capability, not empathy.
"We care deeply" is not a brand position. "We're there when you need us" paired with
proof of capability is.

**Example brands:** Johnson & Johnson, TOMS, Volvo, UNICEF, Kind

**Interesting combinations:**
- Caregiver + Sage = Trusted expertise (WebMD at its best, better healthcare brands)
- Caregiver + Hero = Protective strength (Volvo, security brands done well)
- Caregiver + Everyperson = Community care (credit unions, local brands)

---

### 11. The Ruler
**Core desire:** Control, order, excellence as standard
**Brand belief:** Excellence is not an aspiration — it's the minimum.

**Visual direction:** Structured grids. Limited, disciplined palette (black, white, gold,
navy — never all four). Serif typography with strong hierarchy. Photography: composed,
controlled, aspirational. Minimal but never cold.

**Verbal direction:** Authoritative. Speaks from a position of leadership. Definitive
statements. "The standard" framing. Less is more — says less than it could, implies more.

**Danger zones:** Ruler brands go wrong when they mistake intimidation for authority.
The best Ruler brands are intimidatingly good, not intimidatingly inaccessible.
When Ruler brands start talking down to their customers, they've broken the contract.

**Example brands:** Mercedes-Benz, Rolex, The New Yorker, Hermès, American Express Black

**Interesting combinations:**
- Ruler + Creator = Craft-obsessed authority (Rolex, Leica)
- Ruler + Sage = Knowledge authority (HBR, McKinsey, Bloomberg)
- Ruler + Magician = Transformative power (Apple at its most premium)

---

### 12. The Creator
**Core desire:** Innovation, self-expression, building lasting things
**Brand belief:** Making something great is its own justification.

**Visual direction:** Expressive but intentional. Creative typography choices that
wouldn't occur to a committee. Unique color combinations. Custom illustration where
others use stock. Layouts that show visible craft — asymmetry with purpose, negative
space as element, detail that rewards attention.

**Verbal direction:** Celebrates process and craft. "Built." "Designed." "Crafted."
"Made." Shows the work behind the work. Precise language — the right word, not the
safe word.

**Danger zones:** Creator brands become alienating when they confuse craft with
self-indulgence. The work is for the user, not for the maker. When Creator brands
start designing for their portfolio instead of their customer, they've lost the thread.

**Example brands:** Adobe, Figma, Lego, Moleskine, Teenage Engineering, Notion (in
its design DNA)

**Interesting combinations:**
- Creator + Sage = Knowledge craft (Notion, Roam Research, Obsidian)
- Creator + Ruler = Craft as authority (Leica, Rolls-Royce at its best)
- Creator + Magician = Transformative making (Adobe, Figma — you create magic with it)

## Archetype Combination Chemistry

The most common high-performing combinations and what makes them work:

| Primary | Secondary | The flavor | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creator | Sage | Intelligent craft — builds AND explains with precision | Figma, Notion, Stripe |
| Hero | Jester | Serious about winning, doesn't take itself seriously | Old Spice, early Under Armour |
| Outlaw | Jester | Comic rebellion — tears down the establishment laughing | Liquid Death, Dollar Shave Club |
| Sage | Ruler | Authoritative intelligence — the trusted authority | Bloomberg, The Economist |
| Lover | Creator | Sensory craft — making beautiful things for people who feel | Aesop, Glossier |
| Everyperson | Jester | Warm humor — everyone's welcome, nothing takes itself seriously | Slack (early), Mailchimp |
| Explorer | Creator | Craft built for the wild — tools worthy of adventure | Yeti, Arc'teryx |
| Magician | Creator | Transformation through making — the tool that makes magic | Adobe, Apple |

**Combinations that often fail:**
- Ruler + Jester: Authority undercuts the joke; humor undercuts the authority. Only works if executed at the highest level (Amex does it occasionally).
- Outlaw + Caregiver: Rebellion and nurturing cancel each other out. Requires a very specific context (healthcare disruption brands try this and usually look confused).
- Innocent + Ruler: Simplicity and authority can coexist but the visual language wars constantly.
