# Visual & Brand Critique Heuristics

## How to Use These

Name the heuristic before applying it. "This fails the Squint Test" is ten times more
useful than "the hierarchy feels off" — it gives the user a mental model they can apply
to future work without you.

**Critique structure:**
1. Name the heuristic: "This fails the Competitor Swap Test."
2. Describe the specific failure: not the category — the exact element.
3. State the commercial consequence: what does this cost in real terms?
4. Prescribe the fix: not the direction — the specific change.
5. Show what good looks like: name a real brand or touchpoint.

Never soften with "this is a good start." The point of a critique is to tell the truth
efficiently, not to protect feelings. A founder who gets honest feedback in two minutes
is better served than one who gets validated feedback in twenty.

---

## H1: The 5-Second Test
**Question:** If someone sees this for 5 seconds and looks away, what do they remember?

**The insight:** The first 5 seconds of exposure are almost entirely visual hierarchy and
emotional register. Words barely register. What sticks is: the dominant visual element,
the primary color impression, and one piece of text (usually the headline).

**Apply it:** Cover the design. Wait 5 seconds. Look, then cover again. What did you
retain? That's what your brand is communicating — whether you intended it or not.

**Common failures:**
- The logo is the dominant element. (Nobody cares about your logo on first encounter.
  The logo should be a detail, not the hero.)
- Multiple visual elements compete for dominance at equal weight.
- The headline says what you do, not why it matters to the specific person seeing it.
- The emotional register is "generic professional" — which means the brain filed it
  under "doesn't apply to me" and moved on.

**The commercial consequence:** Every failed 5-second test is a lost first impression.
In paid media, it's a wasted impression. On a landing page, it's a bounce. In a pitch
deck, it's the moment an investor starts checking their email.

---

## H2: The Squint Test
**Question:** If you blur this design, can you still see the structure?

**Apply it:** Squint until the design is unreadable. You should still see: primary,
secondary, and tertiary visual zones. A clear CTA location. Distinct sections.
Balanced composition. If everything becomes the same level of blur-intensity, the
hierarchy is flat — and flat hierarchy means users don't know what to do.

**Common failures:**
- Everything is the same weight and size — the visual equivalent of speaking in a
  monotone.
- Sections bleed into each other without visual separation.
- CTA buttons don't contrast enough to survive the blur.
- The design passes the squint test in desktop mode and fails completely on mobile
  (because mobile rewraps content into a single column of equal-weight elements).

**The commercial consequence:** Poor hierarchy forces users to make decisions about
what to look at — and most won't bother. Conversion drops directly from hierarchy
failures. This is one of the most common and most expensive design problems.

---

## H3: The Screenshot Test
**Question:** If someone screenshots this and sends it with no context, does it work?

**Apply it:** Remove the URL bar. Remove any meta-context (what you know about the
company, what you were told before seeing it). Can you tell: what this product does,
who it's for, what you should do next, and why you should trust it?

**Common failures:**
- Landing page assumes prior brand knowledge. "The future of [thing]" means nothing
  to someone who hasn't heard of you.
- Headline is clever but not clear. Clarity always beats cleverness for cold audiences.
- No trust signals visible above the fold — no logos, numbers, testimonials, or social proof.
- The CTA assumes you know what you're signing up for.

**The commercial consequence:** Every paid acquisition channel sends cold traffic.
Cold traffic has no context. Design that requires context to work is design that fails
on every acquisition channel you pay for.

---

## H4: The Competitor Swap Test
**Question:** Could you replace the logo with a competitor's and it would still work?

**Apply it:** Mentally put a competitor's name and logo on your design. If nothing feels
wrong, you don't have a brand — you have a category placeholder.

**Common failures (in B2B SaaS specifically):**
- Navy blue background with geometric sans-serif and gradient blobs (fintech template 1)
- White background, Inter typeface, "The modern platform for X" headline (template 2)
- Stock photography of diverse professionals looking at laptops (template 3)
- Feature grid showing 6-8 capabilities with icons (every competitor has this section)

**The commercial consequence:** When you look like your competitors, you compete on
price, because there's no other differentiation for the customer to hold onto. This
is the most expensive long-term consequence of weak brand differentiation.

**How to fix it:** Run the test on your 3 biggest competitors' homepages too. Find
one visual OR verbal element that none of them own. Occupy that territory.

## H5: The "So What" Test
**Question:** After reading the headline, does the visitor know why they should care?

**Apply it:** Read the headline aloud. Say "so what?" If the answer isn't built into
the headline itself, it fails. Headlines must convey value, not just describe function.

**The Specificity Ladder in action:**
- "Automated Invoice Processing" → So what? → Rung 1-2
- "Your Finance Team Processes Invoices 10x Faster" → Better, but table stakes → Rung 3
- "Your AP Team Never Touches a Routine Invoice Again" → Specific, falsifiable → Rung 4
- "AP That Runs Itself" → Compressed, ownable → Rung 5

**The commercial consequence:** A headline that fails the "So What" test is a homepage
that produces confused visitors instead of interested prospects. Every point of confusion
is a conversion that didn't happen.

---

## H6: The Consistency Audit
**Question:** Do all touchpoints feel like the same brand made them?

**Apply it:** Open every channel simultaneously: website, app, social media, email
templates, pitch deck, documentation, job listings. The same brand should be visible
in all of them — not identical, but unmistakably related.

**Common failures:**
- Website is polished and modern; app is clearly built by engineers without design input
- Marketing copy is warm and casual; product error messages are cold and corporate
- Social media feels like whoever's on community duty is winging it
- The pitch deck looks like it was built in PowerPoint with default themes
- Job listings read like legal documents from a different company

**The commercial consequence:** Brand inconsistency signals organizational chaos to
sophisticated buyers. A CFO evaluating an AP tool who sees a polished website and then
an ugly app is updating their risk model in real time.

---

## H7: The Cognitive Load Test
**Question:** How many things compete for attention at once?

**Apply it:** Count the number of: distinct calls to action, navigation items, text
blocks above the fold, visual focal points, font sizes in use. If any of these numbers
exceeds 4-5, you have a cognitive load problem.

**The 3-3-3 rule for landing pages:**
- 3 seconds to know what you do
- 3 seconds to know if it's for them
- 3 seconds to know what to do next

If any of these takes longer, there's too much competing.

**Common failures:**
- Navigation with 8+ items (every item is a decision)
- Hero with: headline + subheadline + description paragraph + two CTAs + product
  screenshot + trust badges (six competing objects)
- Feature grids showing 9+ capabilities at once

## H8: The Emotion Test
**Question:** What does this design make you feel — and is it the right feeling?

**Apply it:** Sit with the design for 10 seconds. Don't analyze. Feel. Then name the
emotion. Compare it to the desired brand perception.

**The mismatch taxonomy:**
- Brand wants "trustworthy," actually communicates "boring" (most B2B SaaS)
- Brand wants "premium," actually communicates "trying too hard" (off-brand luxury signals)
- Brand wants "fun," actually communicates "childish" (primary colors + cartoon mascots
  for an adult product)
- Brand wants "serious," actually communicates "intimidating" (heavy black everywhere,
  no warmth)
- Brand wants "innovative," actually communicates "confused" (too many visual languages)

**The diagnosis:** Emotional mismatch usually has one of three root causes: archetype
confusion (the visual and verbal languages don't share a personality), tonal inconsistency
(headline tone and visual tone don't match), or borrowed aesthetics (the brand looks like
another brand in a different category that doesn't have the same meaning in this one).

---

## H9: The Typography Stress Test
**Question:** Does the type system hold up at every scale and content type?

**Apply it:** Find the most extreme content types in the product: hero headline,
body paragraph, data table, error message, mobile navigation label, long-form article,
button text, legal disclaimer. Run every type style through every scenario.

**What breaks:**
- Beautiful display font that's unreadable at body text size
- No hierarchy between H2, H3, and H4 (they're all "medium")
- Buttons and labels using the same style as body text
- Numbers in a sans-serif font that doesn't have real tabular figures (financial data looks wrong)
- Long-form content in a font optimized for UI, not reading

---

## H10: The Cultural Context Test
**Question:** Does this work for the actual audience — or the designer's peer group?

**Apply it:** Describe your audience. Now describe the people who made this design.
Are they the same? If not, did the design process include feedback from actual audience
members — not just stakeholders?

**Common failure patterns:**
- Ultra-minimal B2B SaaS for an audience of 50-year-old procurement managers who
  interpret minimal as "unfinished"
- Glassmorphism and brutalism for conservative enterprise buyers who interpret it as
  untrustworthy
- Extremely casual tone for C-suite buyers who interpret casual as "not serious about security"
- Western design patterns for Southeast Asian markets without adaptation
- Dark mode as the primary experience for audiences that predominantly use devices in
  bright daylight

## H11: The Brand Coherence Test (NEW)
**Question:** Can every design decision be explained by a single core brand truth?

**Apply it:** State your core brand belief in one sentence. Now ask this question of
every element: logo, color palette, typography, imagery style, copy tone, navigation
structure. Does each one flow from that core belief?

**The Stripe example:**
Core truth: "The internet economy deserves infrastructure as good as its best software."
- Clean, precise design → flows (infrastructure that respects craft)
- Developer-first documentation → flows (the builders deserve the best tools)
- Stripe Press (a book publisher) → flows (intellectual depth as infrastructure brand signal)
- Gradient art installations → flows (beauty in the infrastructure layer)

**What incoherence looks like:**
- A brand that says it values simplicity but has a 12-item navigation
- A brand that says it values trust but uses stock photos instead of real team imagery
- A brand that says it's premium but uses Google Forms for customer intake

**The commercial consequence:** Incoherent brands are impossible to build genuine loyalty
toward. Customers can't be faithful to something that doesn't hold a consistent position.
Brand coherence is the foundation of retention, advocacy, and pricing power.

## H12: The Signature Moment Test (NEW)
**Question:** Does the brand have one moment that defines the relationship?

**Apply it:** Name the single touchpoint where your brand delivers its most concentrated
version of itself. Not your best feature — your most distinctively branded interaction.

**Examples of great signature moments:**
- Apple's unboxing (the slowness of the lid lift is a designed ritual)
- Slack's first "magic moment" — the channel fills with messages the second you join
- Mailchimp's "Freddie giving you a high five" before you send a campaign (fear-to-excitement)
- Stripe's first successful charge notification to developers
- Amazon's "Your item is out for delivery" precision

**What it reveals:**
Brands without a signature moment have no concentrated brand experience. The customer's
relationship is diffuse — vaguely positive or vaguely negative, but nothing they'd tell
a story about. The signature moment is the story they tell.

**The prescription:** Name the moment the customer is most emotionally present during
their interaction with the product. Design for that moment first, not last.

---

## H13: The Enemy Test (NEW)
**Question:** Does this brand know what it's against?

**Apply it:** Read the copy and visual direction without seeing the brand name. Can you
tell what philosophy, behavior, or category assumption this brand exists to fight?

**Signs a brand knows its enemy:**
- Copywriting has a clear point of view on what's wrong in the world
- Visual direction makes an implicit statement about what the brand refuses to look like
- Every design decision is a small act of differentiation from the category default

**Signs a brand has no enemy:**
- The messaging could have been written by the industry association
- The design looks like the average of all competitors
- There's nothing on the homepage that would make a competitor uncomfortable

**The commercial consequence:** Brands without a clear enemy have no natural advocates.
People don't refer things they're neutral about. They refer things that articulate a
belief they hold. Your enemy is the thing your best customers hate — name it and you
recruit them as evangelists.

---

## H14: The Thumbnail Test (NEW)
**Question:** Does this work at 100×100 pixels?

**Apply it:** Export the brand mark or key visual at 100×100 pixels. View it as a
profile photo, a favicon, a social media avatar. Is it still recognizable? Is it still
distinctive?

**What fails at small scale:**
- Wordmarks with thin letterforms (vanish at small sizes)
- Logomarks with too much detail (become blobs)
- Brands that rely entirely on color when viewed on different screen calibrations
- Typography-only brands with no visual anchor

**Why this matters beyond logos:**
Social media thumbnails, app icons, browser tabs, notification icons, email sender
avatars — these are often the first brand exposure a returning user has. If the
brand mark doesn't work here, the brand is fighting itself.

---

## The Priority Hierarchy

When a brand fails multiple heuristics, fix in this order:

1. **H4 (Competitor Swap)** — if you're indistinguishable, nothing else matters
2. **H1 (5-Second Test)** — first impression is first opportunity
3. **H5 ("So What")** — clarity before conversion
4. **H11 (Brand Coherence)** — the foundation everything else depends on
5. **H2 (Squint Test)** — hierarchy enables everything downstream
6. **H8 (Emotion Test)** — if the feeling is wrong, the execution is wrong
7. Everything else — important, but these six are existential
