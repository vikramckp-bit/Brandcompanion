# Rebranding Protocol

## When Rebranding Is the Right Move

First: establish whether this is actually a rebranding problem. Most teams reach for
the rebrand when the problem is actually positioning, product, or culture. A new logo
won't fix those.

**Rebrand if:**
- The company has fundamentally changed what it does or who it serves
- The current brand is actively preventing growth (enterprise prospects feel the consumer brand
  is too casual; or a new B2C audience finds the enterprise brand intimidating)
- The brand name has a meaning problem (impossible to say, trademarked, offensive in a new market)
- You're entering a new category where the current brand would be a liability
- The brand has become invisible — it no longer says anything because it never said anything

**Don't rebrand if:**
- The product hasn't changed but you're bored of the look
- A new CMO wants to make their mark
- You've raised a round and feel like you should look different now
- Your team doesn't like the font

**The rebrand conversation starter:**
> "Before we talk about what the new brand should look like, tell me what the current brand
> is stopping you from doing. Who's saying no because of how you look or sound — and what
> would they say yes to?"

## Phase 1: The Brand Audit

Don't design anything until you've audited what exists. What you're about to replace has
equity — some of it hidden, some of it wrong. Your job is to find it before you destroy it.

### The Equity Inventory

Map every piece of the current brand against three questions:

| Element | Is it recognized? | Is it liked? | Is it right? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand name | [Yes/No/Sometimes] | [Yes/No/Mixed] | [Yes/No/Needs evolution] |
| Logo/wordmark | | | |
| Primary color | | | |
| Typography | | | |
| Photography style | | | |
| Brand voice/tone | | | |
| Tagline | | | |
| Core messaging | | | |
| Brand personality | | | |

**How to score:**
- **Recognized:** Do customers, prospects, and partners associate this element with the brand?
  Even if they can't name it, do they notice when it changes?
- **Liked:** When customers encounter this element, does it create a positive or neutral response?
  Negative responses are easy to identify (complaints). Neutral is the dangerous one — it means
  the element has no equity at all.
- **Right:** Does this element accurately represent what the company is today and where it's going?
  An element can be recognized and liked but wrong if the company has moved on.

### The Survival Matrix

Score each element:

| Score | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Recognized + Liked + Right | Core equity — protect this | Evolve, don't replace |
| Recognized + Liked + Wrong | Legacy equity — handle carefully | Transition with narrative |
| Recognized + Disliked + Wrong | Liability — move fast | Replace with explanation |
| Not recognized + anything | No equity — free to change | Replace without ceremony |

**The classic mistake:** Killing what's recognized because the team is bored of it.
Equity you have is worth more than equity you hope to build. The bar for replacing
a recognized element must be very high.

## Phase 2: The Pivot Diagnosis

Understand WHY the rebrand is happening. The reason determines how radical the change
must be and how to narrate it.

### Rebranding Archetypes

**Type 1: The Evolution**
Company is the same, but has grown up. The brand needs to signal maturity, expanded
capabilities, or a higher tier of customer without abandoning the community that got
you here.

*Examples:* Mailchimp's 2018 rebrand (from scrappy email tool to marketing platform).
Slack's gradual tonal shift from startup-casual to enterprise-capable.

*Approach:* Retain the recognizable equity. Elevate the visual language. Don't explain
yourself — just be more. The change should feel inevitable in retrospect.

*Danger:* Moving too far from the community that built the brand. Every Mailchimp user
who said "they got corporate on us" is a potential churn.

**Type 2: The Pivot**
The ICP, category, or business model has changed. The brand built for the old reality
is now wrong for the new one.

*Examples:* Instagram (photo filter app → cultural platform). Slack (internal gaming
communication tool → work communication). Notion (personal notes → team collaboration).

*Approach:* Identify the through-line from old to new. The rebrand works if you can
tell a coherent story: "We were always about [X]. Now that we serve [new audience/use
case], [X] means [this]." Without that narrative, the rebrand feels like abandonment.

*Danger:* Losing the early community without winning the new one during the transition.

**Type 3: The Clean Break**
The old brand is a liability — negative association, wrong category, or no recognition
to speak of. Start fresh.

*Examples:* Valuetainment becoming something legitimate. A company escaping a
controversy by renaming. A B2C brand repositioning to enterprise where the old
identity signals the wrong thing.

*Approach:* Move fast, explain once, and let the new work speak. Don't linger on the
old brand — every mention reinforces the association you're trying to replace.

*Danger:* Customers who loved the old brand feel abandoned. If there's an existing
community, the clean break requires a very honest "why" or it will feel like betrayal.

**Type 4: The Expansion**
Brand is fine but needs to stretch into new territory — new markets, new products,
new categories. The core brand must evolve to accommodate the expansion without
losing coherence.

*Examples:* Apple expanding from Mac → iPod → iPhone → Services. Amazon expanding
from books → everything → AWS → media. Google becoming Alphabet.

*Approach:* The parent brand must become more abstract — big enough to contain the
expansion. Sub-brands or product lines can carry more specific personalities.

## Phase 3: The Transition Architecture

### The Narrative

Every rebrand needs a public narrative. Without one, customers, employees, and press
will write their own — usually the worst possible interpretation.

**The narrative structure:**
1. **Acknowledgment:** What we've been, what we've built, what we're proud of
2. **The reason:** What changed — externally or internally — that makes this the right moment
3. **The evolution:** What stays (the belief, the mission, the community) and what changes (the expression)
4. **The future:** What this makes possible that wasn't possible before
5. **The ask:** Come with us

What to avoid: "New look, same great [product]" — this is the narrative of a company
that doesn't believe in its own change. Also avoid: over-explaining the design decisions
(nobody cares why you chose this typeface).

### The Rollout Sequence

| Phase | Timeframe | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal first** | 2-4 weeks before launch | Team understands and believes in the new brand. They're the first ambassadors. |
| **Close community** | 1-2 weeks before launch | Key customers, partners, investors see it before the public. They feel special. They become advocates. |
| **Soft launch** | Launch day | New brand live. Founder narrative published. Story ready. |
| **Press + amplification** | Week 1 | Pitch the "why now" story, not the "what changed" story. |
| **Total migration** | Weeks 2-8 | Old assets replaced. No mixed-brand experiences. |

**The hardest part:** The mixed-brand period. During migration, old and new brand will
coexist in different touchpoints. This is worse than either one individually because it
signals disorganization. Set a hard deadline for total migration and meet it.

### The Equity Transition Rules

1. **If an element has equity (recognized + liked), transition with ceremony.** Don't
   just replace it. Acknowledge it. "We've updated our [X] to [Y]." Let loyal users
   see that you know what you're changing.

2. **If an element has no equity, replace without ceremony.** Don't draw attention to
   things nobody noticed.

3. **Never rebrand the thing customers love about your product.** The brand can change.
   The core product experience must be consistent. Mailchimp changed its visual brand
   significantly; Freddie the chimp survived because he had genuine affection.

## Phase 4: The New Brand Direction

After completing phases 1-3, proceed to `modules/discovery.md` with the following
rebranding-specific frame:

**Questions that differ from a greenfield brand build:**

1. **What's the through-line?** What belief does the old brand and new brand share?
   If there's no through-line, this is a Clean Break — and you should say so.

2. **What's the emotional territory to evolve from vs. to?**
   Map current perception → desired perception on the 5-axis scale. The distance
   between the two determines how dramatic the visual change must be.

3. **What equity element is non-negotiable?** Even in a Clean Break, there's usually
   one element — a color, a wordmark treatment, a brand voice quality — that the
   community is attached to. Find it. Protect it if possible.

4. **Who's the customer whose trust you can't afford to lose?**
   Every rebrand has a constituency that could become enemies if mishandled.
   Name them. Design the transition narrative for them specifically.

## The Rebrand Risk Assessment

Before executing, score these risks:

| Risk | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Community size and passion | Small / not vocal | Medium / semi-engaged | Large / highly engaged |
| Brand recognition | Minimal | Moderate | Strong |
| Current brand sentiment | Negative | Neutral | Positive |
| Change magnitude | Evolutionary | Significant | Total break |
| Transition narrative strength | Compelling | Adequate | Weak |

High scores in Community + Recognition + Positive Sentiment = proceed with extreme care.
This brand has something worth protecting. Move deliberately.

High scores in Change Magnitude + Weak Narrative = significant risk. Before launching,
strengthen the narrative or reduce the change magnitude.

The most dangerous combination: A loved brand making a dramatic change without a
compelling story for why. This is how brands lose their community in a weekend.
